A · The host venue

What hosting a bartender means

You lend your bar to a guest for one night. Your licence, your house rules and your team stay in charge; the guest brings the menu and the energy.

The venue provides

  • One night, with the bar open to the guest from 6 pm to 10 pm (later if you like)
  • An opening order of the brand before the night, so the menu can be poured and stays on after
  • The ingredients for the guest menu, bought by the venue from the bartender's mise en place list
  • A run-through the day before with the guest: check every ingredient is in and prep any complicated garnishes
  • A duty manager and bar back to induct the guest and work alongside them
  • A station, ice, glassware, garnish prep space and POS buttons for the menu
  • The guest menu printed or on your table cards for the night
  • Promotion to your own guests and socials
  • Service under your licence and RSA rules, as on any other night

The venue receives

  • Pre-sold tickets: revenue for two cocktails per guest before the doors open
  • A guest bartender and a menu built for your room
  • The full menu and mise en place list at least two weeks before the guest arrives
  • A ticketed crowd from the Drinks Network campaign
  • Every other drink and plate sold on the night
  • Trade buyers in the room, hosted on the brand
  • Event artwork, captions and posts with your venue front and centre
  • No ticketing admin

C · The bartender

Your menu. Their bar.

A guest shift in a new city, with your name on the poster and your drinks on the menu.

The bartender provides

  • A two to three drink menu built around the brand, quick to make on a busy night
  • The menu and mise en place list to the venue at least two weeks before arriving: every spirit, modifier, syrup, garnish and piece of glassware
  • A run-through with the venue the day before, plus prep for any complicated garnishes
  • A 6 pm to 10 pm shift, working under the venue's duty manager and house rules
  • An RSA that's valid in the host state
  • Time for photos, a short video and a chat with trade buyers
  • A short story for each drink, for the menu and the posts

The bartender receives

  • Return flights and accommodation, paid by the brand
  • A $500 stipend, and a fee of up to $1,000 on some takeovers
  • A profile, poster and posts across Drinks Network and the venue
  • A new city's bar scene, and the people in it

Questions

Straight answers.

Does it have to be a capital city?

No. A takeover can run anywhere with a good bar and people who want a night out, from Sydney, Melbourne and Brisbane to Ballarat or Wollongong.

Does the venue have to stock the brand?

Yes. Taking the product on is part of the program. The venue places an opening order before the night, so the guest menu can be poured and the drinks stay on the list after the guest has gone.

Who buys the ingredients for the guest menu?

The venue. The bartender sends the menu and a full mise en place list at least two weeks before arriving, and the venue buys everything on it. The two meet the day before the shift to check nothing's missing and to prep any complicated garnishes ahead of time.

Who is responsible for service on the night?

The venue. The guest works under the venue's licence, duty manager and house rules, like any other shift, and needs an RSA that's valid in the host state.

What does a ticket include?

Two cocktails from the guest bartender's menu during their shift. Anything else is bought at the bar as normal, and those sales are the venue's.

How does the brand get the ticket-holder contacts?

Guests tick a box at checkout to share their details with the brand. Only the people who tick it are passed on.

Is the bartender a prize winner?

No. It's a paid working trip. The brand covers flights, accommodation and a $500 stipend, and some takeovers add a fee of up to $1,000.
