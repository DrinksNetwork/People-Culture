When Your Brand Becomes the Headline: Death by Counterfeit Alcohol

By Hayden Wood · 13 August 2025

Picture this: you’re halfway through your morning coffee when your phone lights up. News alerts. Three people in Cambodia are dead. Two more in ICU. The drink that killed them? Something with your label on it

Except it wasn’t yours_____

Welcome to the nightmare every spirits and wine producer hopes never to face — your brand name splashed across headlines next to words like methanol poisoning and death toll. And in Southeast Asia, it’s not an “if” but a “when” for many big names.

Cloudy Bay knock-offs in Shanghai. Johnnie Walker, Chivas Regal, and Jack Daniel’s fakes everywhere from Vietnam to Laos. Louis Tre Cognac imitations that even seasoned drinkers couldn’t spot without a lab test. The counterfeiters have range — and they’re not just playing with branding, they’re playing with body counts.

The stakes?

Beyond the obvious human tragedy, it’s brand trust — gone overnight. Your years of marketing, your carefully built premium positioning, your story — all overshadowed by a single headline that makes you sound like you’re brewing poison in a bathtub. Shareholders get nervous. Distributors start pulling back. Sales take a hit, not just in the affected country, but globally.

How do you handle it?

Here’s the uncomfortable truth: most brands aren’t ready. Crisis media plans, if they exist at all, are generic and slow. Local distributors scramble, mutter something about “isolated incidents,” and hope it blows over. Meanwhile, the story’s already halfway around the world.

What you should have in place before it happens

  • Rapid-response PR team with local language capability — hours, not days.
  • Distributor protocols to quarantine and investigate suspect stock immediately.
  • Clear communication lines with embassies and local authorities (even the ones you don’t fully trust).
  • Victim support statements — yes, even if you weren’t responsible.

 

The Alternative Script: How to Kind of Pretend it Never Happened

Now, here’s how things often go when disaster strikes—and the brand is the one tagged in the headlines—even if it wasn’t their product:

1

Run Your "Tests"—Sort Of

Four days after the Warwick Resort incident, Fiji’s government breathlessly announces that testing on the cocktails showed no methanol. Alcohol poisoning? Nope. Fully innocent. They shipped samples to New Zealand three months later—but conveniently didn’t send the one from the sickest patient, “Susan,” for analysis.
No transparency. No accountability.

The unspoken problem is that in regions like Southeast Asia, counterfeit is so ingrained — 47% of some categories in Vietnam are illicit — that you could have a perfect supply chain and still get hit. And when that happens, nobody cares about your quality control or authenticity measures. They care about the fact someone’s lying in a morgue with your logo on the bottle.

It’s a grim truth, but the sooner brands accept that managing the fallout is just as important as preventing the crime, the better their odds of surviving the headline. Because in this market, you’re not just selling a drink — you’re selling the promise that it won’t kill you. And that’s a promise worth protecting.
