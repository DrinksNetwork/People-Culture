How to Counterfeit Liquor and Why It Works (Hint: Because No One’s Stopping You)

By Hayden Wood · 15 August 2025

Let’s be honest — if counterfeiting liquor were a sport, Southeast Asia would be the Olympic Games. It’s been going on for decades, and lately, it’s entered the digital age like a kid with a new iPhone.

Labels? AI can replicate them in an afternoon. Bottles? Cheaper than chips from glass plants who don’t ask questions. Fake caps? There’s a factory in Guangdong that’ll ship them to you in bulk.

You don’t need to be Pablo Escobar to get in on it.

If you’ve got a bit of start-up capital, a warehouse that doesn’t smell too much like a sewer, and a willingness to risk killing someone for profit, you’re in business. It’s that simple.

The Counterfeiter’s Starter Kit:

  • Label Design: Download a JPEG of Johnnie Walker. AI upscales it. Done.
  • Bottles: Order a container load from China. They’ll even add punt marks to make them look premium.
  • Caps & Seals: Available on Alibaba. Ships in 10 days.
  • The Liquid: Grain alcohol cut with water, caramel colouring, and a dash of whatever passes for whisky essence in your part of the world.
  • Production Run: 50,000 bottles a batch is not unusual.

That’s why we now have 47% of certain liquor categories in Vietnam being illicit or unrecorded. Yes — almost half the market. And the real kicker? Most drinkers can’t tell the difference until they wake up blind, vomiting, or dead.

The Hot Spots

  • Vietnam: The crown jewel. Long history, massive demand, and enough small bars and street kiosks to hide the trade in plain sight.
  • Cambodia & Laos: Popular with tourists who wouldn’t spot a fake label if it bit them.
  • Indonesia: Produces its own illegal spirits and imports fakes from neighbours.
  • Myanmar: Untouched by enforcement — a playground for counterfeiters.

How They Get Away With It

  • Corruption & Complicity: You don’t move 15,000 cases across a border without “clipping the ticket.”
  • Weak Enforcement: Laws exist, but fines and sentences are a joke compared to profits.
  • Tourist Apathy: Backpackers chasing cheap drinks aren’t asking about provenance.
  • Local Demand: Poorer consumers see counterfeits as “good enough” luxury.

Case Study: Rex D’Aquino – Australia’s Fake Booze Kingpin

Rex D’Aquino built a lucrative business blending and bottling counterfeit spirits—everything from “Scotch” that never saw Scotland to knock-off tequila. His companies, including Fernbrew Pty Ltd, pumped this fake liquor into the market for years, undercutting legitimate producers and dodging millions in excise tax.

The consequences? A record A$87 million tax bill (likely much higher), multiple Federal Court injunctions banning him from ever selling “Scotch” again, and the collapse of his businesses into liquidation. While he avoided jail, the reputational damage was permanent—his name is now shorthand in the industry for how far counterfeiters can go before the law catches up.

Why It Works (and Will Keep Working)

Because the rewards massively outweigh the risks. You can make ten times your money per bottle. Get caught? Worst case, you pay a fine smaller than a weekend’s profits. Best case, you bribe your way out and are back in business the same week.

Meanwhile, legitimate producers watch their market share erode and their brands get linked to poisonings they had nothing to do with. Australian distillers like to think this is “someone else’s problem” — but tell that to Melbourne’s $2 billion illicit alcohol seizure last year. If that had been legitimate, the excise tax alone would have been worth millions.

Bottom line: If you’re shocked by how brazen this is, you haven’t been paying attention. In Southeast Asia, counterfeiting liquor isn’t just an industry — it’s a tradition. And unless the risks start to outweigh the rewards, the only thing changing will be the quality of the fakes. Spoiler: they’re getting better.
